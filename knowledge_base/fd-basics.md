# Fixed Deposits (FD) — Complete Basics

## What is a Fixed Deposit?

A Fixed Deposit is a financial product where you deposit a lump sum of money with a bank or NBFC (Non-Banking Financial Company) for a fixed period of time, in exchange for a fixed, guaranteed interest rate. Unlike a SIP or mutual fund, the return is locked in when you open the FD and does not change with market movements — this predictability is the main reason people choose FDs.

## How it works

1. You choose an amount and a tenure (ranging from as short as 7 days to as long as 10 years, depending on the bank).
2. The bank quotes you an interest rate for that specific tenure — rates differ by tenure and by bank, and change periodically based on RBI's monetary policy.
3. Your money is locked in for that period. At maturity, you get back your principal plus the interest earned.
4. You can choose how interest is paid out — either periodically (monthly/quarterly, called a "non-cumulative" FD) or all at once at maturity along with the principal (called a "cumulative" FD, where interest also compounds).

## Types of FDs

- **Regular FD** — the standard version described above.
- **Tax-saving FD** — a special 5-year FD that qualifies for a tax deduction (covered below), but comes with a mandatory 5-year lock-in with no premature withdrawal allowed.
- **Senior Citizen FD** — banks typically offer a higher interest rate (commonly about 0.25%–0.50% more) to senior citizens on the same tenure.
- **Cumulative FD** — interest compounds and is paid out only at maturity along with the principal. Better for growing a lump sum.
- **Non-cumulative FD** — interest is paid out at regular intervals (monthly/quarterly/annually), useful for people who want a regular income stream from their deposit.
- **Flexi/Sweep-in FD** — linked to your savings account; if your savings balance exceeds a set limit, the excess automatically moves into an FD, and money can be pulled back automatically if your savings account runs short. Useful for earning better interest on idle savings-account money while keeping liquidity.
- **NRE/NRO FD** — for Non-Resident Indians, with different tax treatment (NRE FD interest is generally tax-exempt in India; NRO FD interest is taxable).

## Premature withdrawal

Most FDs allow you to withdraw before maturity, but usually with a penalty — typically a reduction in the interest rate you actually earn (e.g., you get the rate applicable for the period you actually held it, minus a penalty of around 0.5%–1%, rather than the original quoted rate). Tax-saving FDs are an exception — they generally do not allow premature withdrawal at all due to the mandatory 5-year lock-in.

## FD laddering (a common strategy)

Instead of putting all your money into one FD with one maturity date, you split it across multiple FDs with staggered maturity dates (e.g., 1 year, 2 years, 3 years). This gives you periodic access to some of your money without breaking the whole deposit, while still benefiting from potentially better rates on longer tenures for the rest.

## How FD interest is taxed

- FD interest is **fully taxable**, added to your total income and taxed at your income tax slab rate — it does not get any special lower "capital gains" rate the way mutual fund gains can.
- **TDS (Tax Deducted at Source):** banks deduct TDS if your total FD interest from that bank exceeds a threshold in a financial year. *(Verified as of September 2026 — this was revised in the 2025 Budget, effective FY 2025-26 onward.)* The current thresholds are **₹50,000 for regular individuals** and **₹1,00,000 for senior citizens** (raised from the earlier ₹40,000/₹50,000 — if you see those older figures anywhere, they're outdated). TDS is deducted at 10% if you've submitted your PAN (20% if you haven't). This provision was Section 194A under the old Income Tax Act, 1961; from 1 April 2026 it's referenced as **Section 393** under the new Income Tax Act, 2025 — same mechanism, new section number. Note: TDS is deducted whether or not your total income is actually taxable — if your income is below the taxable limit, you can avoid the deduction by submitting Form 15G (or Form 15H for senior citizens) to the bank in advance.
- TDS is not the final tax — you still report the full interest as income when filing your return, and either pay additional tax if your slab rate is higher than the TDS rate, or claim a refund if it's lower.

## Tax-saving FD (5-year FD)

- Qualifies for a deduction of **up to ₹1.5 lakh per financial year**, under what was Section 80C of the old Income Tax Act (now **Section 123** under the Income Tax Act, 2025 effective from 1 April 2026 — same limit, shared across all 80C/123-type instruments like PPF, ELSS, insurance, etc., not a separate limit).
- Only available under the **old tax regime**.
- Comes with a **mandatory 5-year lock-in** and generally does not allow premature withdrawal.
- Unlike ELSS (3-year lock-in, potentially higher returns since it's equity-based), a tax-saving FD offers guaranteed but generally lower returns with a longer lock-in — a common comparison point beginners ask about.
- Important: the interest earned on a tax-saving FD is still fully taxable every year, even though the principal investment got you a deduction. Only the investment amount is deductible, not the interest.

## Deposit insurance — is FD money "safe"?

Bank deposits (including FDs) in India are insured by **DICGC (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation)**, a subsidiary of RBI, up to **₹5 lakh per depositor per bank** (covering principal plus interest combined) — meaning if a bank fails, you're guaranteed to get back up to ₹5 lakh, but any amount above that per bank is not insured. This is why some people spread large FD amounts across multiple banks rather than keeping everything in one. Note: this DICGC cover applies to bank deposits; FDs with NBFCs (non-bank finance companies) do not carry this same government-backed insurance, which is an important distinction when comparing "bank FD" vs "company/NBFC FD" offers that advertise higher rates.

## FD vs other options (quick comparisons)

- **FD vs Debt Mutual Fund:** FD gives a guaranteed fixed rate; debt funds have variable, market-linked returns and carry some risk, but historically debt funds have sometimes offered better post-tax returns for money not needed for a few years — worth comparing on a post-tax basis, not just headline rate.
- **FD vs Recurring Deposit (RD):** FD is a one-time lump sum; RD is a fixed amount deposited monthly (similar structure to a SIP, but into a bank RD instead of a mutual fund) — both offer guaranteed, fixed interest.
- **FD vs Savings Account:** FD offers a meaningfully higher interest rate than a regular savings account, in exchange for locking the money away for a fixed period.

## Common beginner questions

**Can I take a loan against my FD?**
Yes, most banks allow you to borrow against an existing FD (usually up to 90–95% of its value) at a rate slightly higher than the FD's own interest rate — often cheaper than a personal loan, and you don't have to break the FD.

**Does the FD interest rate change during the tenure?**
No — for a regular fixed-rate FD, the rate you're quoted at booking stays locked for the full tenure, regardless of how interest rates in the economy move afterward. (Some banks also offer floating-rate FDs, which are less common and do fluctuate.)

**What happens automatically at maturity?**
Depends on the instructions you gave — the bank will either pay out the maturity amount to your linked account, or auto-renew the FD for the same tenure at the then-prevailing rate, based on what you selected when opening it.
