# Income Tax Basics — Complete Guide

## How income tax actually works in India

India uses a **progressive slab system** — different portions of your income are taxed at different rates, not your entire income at one flat rate. As your income rises, only the portion within each higher slab gets taxed at that slab's rate; you don't suddenly pay a higher rate on your *entire* income just by crossing into a new slab.

## Old regime vs New regime — the core choice

Every taxpayer can choose between two systems each year:

- **New tax regime (default since FY 2023-24):** lower tax rates across more slabs, but very few deductions/exemptions allowed (no 80C, 80D, HRA in most cases, home loan interest on self-occupied property, etc.). Simpler, less paperwork.
- **Old tax regime:** higher rates, but allows the full range of deductions covered throughout these articles (80C/123 for SIP/PPF/ELSS/insurance principal, 80D/126 for health insurance, home loan interest, HRA, and more).

**Which is better depends entirely on how many deductions you actually claim.** If you have substantial 80C investments, health insurance, and a home loan, the old regime can work out cheaper despite higher headline rates. If you claim few or no deductions, the new regime's lower rates usually win. There's no universally correct answer — it needs to be calculated for each person's specific numbers, ideally using an online calculator with your actual income and deduction figures.

## New tax regime slabs (verified as of September 2026, FY 2026-27 — unchanged from FY 2025-26 per Budget 2026)

| Income range | Tax rate |
|---|---|
| Up to ₹4,00,000 | Nil |
| ₹4,00,000 – ₹8,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹8,00,000 – ₹12,00,000 | 10% |
| ₹12,00,000 – ₹16,00,000 | 15% |
| ₹16,00,000 – ₹20,00,000 | 20% |
| ₹20,00,000 – ₹24,00,000 | 25% |
| Above ₹24,00,000 | 30% |

## Old tax regime slabs (for individuals under 60)

| Income range | Tax rate |
|---|---|
| Up to ₹2,50,000 | Nil |
| ₹2,50,000 – ₹5,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹5,00,000 – ₹10,00,000 | 20% |
| Above ₹10,00,000 | 30% |

Senior citizens (60+) and super senior citizens (80+) get a higher basic exemption limit (₹3 lakh and ₹5 lakh respectively) under the **old regime only** — the new regime's exemption limit doesn't change based on age.

## The rebate that makes income effectively tax-free up to a higher threshold

Under **Section 87A**, taxpayers in the new regime with total taxable income up to a certain threshold get a rebate that brings their tax liability to **zero** — combined with the standard deduction (₹75,000 for salaried individuals under the new regime), this effectively makes income **up to roughly ₹12-12.75 lakh tax-free** for many salaried taxpayers under the new regime, even though the slab table above shows tax starting at ₹4 lakh. This rebate is a separate mechanism from the slabs themselves — it's worth understanding both pieces, since headlines often mention just "tax-free up to ₹12 lakh" without explaining that it comes from this rebate, not from the slabs directly.

## The new Income Tax Act, 2025

From **1 April 2026**, the **Income Tax Act, 2025** replaced the older Income Tax Act, 1961. This was primarily a **renumbering and simplification exercise**, not a change to slab rates or major rules — many familiar sections got new numbers (Section 80C became Section 123, Section 80D became Section 126, Section 80E became Section 129, and so on, as covered in the other articles). If you see an older article referencing "Section 80C" and a newer one referencing "Section 123," they're generally describing the same rule under different Act numbering — worth noting when a user asks why numbers seem inconsistent across sources.

## Key deductions available (old regime only, unless noted)

- **Section 80C/123** — up to ₹1.5 lakh combined across PPF, EPF, ELSS, life insurance premium, home loan principal, tax-saving FD, and more (see the SIP, FD, and Retirement articles for details).
- **Section 80D/126** — health insurance premiums, up to ₹1,00,000 combined for self/family and senior citizen parents (see the Insurance article).
- **Section 80CCD(1B)** — additional ₹50,000 for NPS Tier 1 (see the Retirement article).
- **Section 80CCD(2)** — employer NPS contributions, available in **both** regimes (see the Retirement article).
- **Section 24(b)** — home loan interest, up to ₹2 lakh for self-occupied property (see the Loans article).
- **Section 80E/129** — education loan interest, no upper limit (see the Loans article).
- **Standard deduction** — ₹75,000 for salaried individuals under the new regime (a different, lower figure applies under the old regime).

## Common misconceptions

- "New regime is always better because rates are lower" — not necessarily true if you have significant deductions; it depends on your specific numbers.
- "Income up to ₹12 lakh isn't taxed under the slabs" — technically the slabs do apply from ₹4 lakh onward; it's the separate 87A rebate that brings the *net tax payable* to zero up to the rebate threshold. Above that threshold, tax is calculated on the full slab structure, not just the amount exceeding ₹12 lakh.
- "You can mix old and new regime benefits" — no, you choose one regime for the full financial year (salaried employees can typically switch each year when filing; those with business income have more restricted switching rules).
- "Filing an ITR is only for people who owe tax" — many people file even when their tax liability is zero, since it's often required for loan applications, visa processing, and is generally good financial practice.
