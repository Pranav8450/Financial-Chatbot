# Mutual Funds — Complete Basics

## What is a mutual fund?

A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it collectively in stocks, bonds, or other securities, managed by a professional fund manager. Instead of you picking individual stocks yourself, you buy "units" of the fund, and the fund manager decides what to buy and sell inside it based on the fund's stated strategy.

Each investor owns a proportional share of the fund based on how many units they hold. When the fund's investments do well, the value of your units goes up; when they don't, it goes down.

## Who runs it

- **AMC (Asset Management Company)** — the company that creates and manages the fund (e.g., a fund house). This is who you're trusting with your money.
- **Fund Manager** — the person/team at the AMC who actually decides what the fund buys and sells, within the fund's stated objective.
- **SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)** — the regulator that sets the rules AMCs must follow, to protect investors.
- **AMFI (Association of Mutual Funds in India)** — an industry body that sets standards and runs investor-awareness programs; not a regulator itself.

## How NAV (Net Asset Value) works

NAV is the price of one unit of a mutual fund, calculated at the end of each trading day based on the total value of everything the fund holds, divided by the number of units. When you invest, you buy units at that day's NAV; when you redeem, you sell at that day's NAV.

**Common misconception:** a lower NAV does NOT mean a fund is "cheaper" or a better deal, and a higher NAV doesn't mean it's expensive. NAV is just a unit price — what matters is the fund's percentage growth over time, not the NAV number itself. A fund with NAV ₹10 and one with NAV ₹500 can both double in value; the NAV number alone tells you nothing about future returns.

## Types of mutual funds, by what they invest in

- **Equity funds** — invest mainly in company stocks. Higher risk, higher potential return, best suited for long-term goals (5+ years).
- **Debt funds** — invest in bonds, government securities, and other fixed-income instruments. Lower risk than equity, more stable, suited for shorter-term goals.
- **Hybrid funds** — mix of equity and debt in varying proportions, aiming to balance growth and stability. Sub-types include conservative hybrid (mostly debt), balanced/aggressive hybrid (more equity), and dynamic asset allocation funds (which shift the mix based on market conditions).
- **Solution-oriented funds** — built around a specific goal, like retirement or children's education, usually with a lock-in period.
- **Other categories** — gold funds (invest in gold, without needing to buy physical gold), and international/global funds (invest in foreign stock markets for geographic diversification).

## Equity fund sub-categories (by company size and strategy)

- **Large-cap** — invest in the biggest, most established companies. Relatively more stable within equity.
- **Mid-cap** — mid-sized companies, higher growth potential but more volatile than large-cap.
- **Small-cap** — smaller companies, highest growth potential but also highest volatility and risk.
- **Multi-cap / Flexi-cap** — invest across large, mid, and small companies, giving the fund manager flexibility to shift based on opportunity.
- **Sectoral/Thematic funds** — focus on one sector (like banking or IT) or theme. Higher risk since there's no diversification across sectors.
- **Index funds** — simply track a market index (like Nifty 50) rather than having a manager actively pick stocks. Typically lower cost since there's less active decision-making involved.

## Debt fund sub-categories (by what they hold and how long)

- **Overnight/Liquid funds** — invest in very short-term instruments (a day to a few weeks). Very low risk, used for parking money briefly.
- **Money market / Ultra-short duration funds** — instruments maturing within about a year. Good for short-term goals.
- **Short/Medium duration funds** — lend for roughly one to four years, balancing yield and stability.
- **Corporate bond funds** — lend mainly to strong-rated companies for relatively higher yield than government securities.
- **Gilt funds** — invest only in government securities, so there's no credit/default risk (government isn't expected to default), though the price can still move with interest rate changes.
- **Credit risk funds** — invest in lower-rated corporate bonds for higher yield, at higher risk of default.

## Open-ended vs Closed-ended funds

- **Open-ended funds** — you can buy or sell units on any business day. Most mutual funds, and virtually all SIP-friendly funds, are open-ended.
- **Closed-ended funds** — have a fixed tenure and a limited window to invest (like a New Fund Offer), and typically can only be redeemed at maturity or traded on a stock exchange in the meantime. Far less common and generally less relevant for a beginner.

## Direct plan vs Regular plan

Every mutual fund scheme is usually available in two versions:

- **Direct plan** — you invest directly with the AMC (via their website/app or a direct platform), with no intermediary commission built in. Lower expense ratio, higher returns over time, purely because less of your money goes to distributor commissions.
- **Regular plan** — you invest through a distributor/advisor/broker, who earns a commission from the AMC (paid out of the fund's expenses, not as a separate fee to you directly). Slightly higher expense ratio as a result.

Over long periods, the small difference in expense ratio between direct and regular plans compounds into a meaningfully different final amount — this is one of the most impactful, easy things a beginner can get right from day one.

## Expense ratio

This is the annual fee the AMC charges to manage the fund, expressed as a percentage of your investment, deducted automatically from the fund's returns (you don't pay it separately — it's already reflected in the NAV/returns you see).

*(Verified as of September 2026 — SEBI overhauled this framework effective 1 April 2026.)* SEBI caps what AMCs can charge, with limits set in slabs that step down as a fund's total assets (AUM) grow — larger funds must pass on cost efficiencies to investors. As of the new framework, the top slab (funds with AUM under ₹500 crore) allows a maximum Base Expense Ratio of around **2.10% for equity-oriented schemes**, **1.85% for debt schemes**, and as low as **0.90% for index funds/ETFs** (index funds are cheaper because there's no active stock-picking involved). Since April 2026, SEBI also requires the fee to be split into three clearly disclosed parts — the core management fee (Base Expense Ratio), brokerage/transaction costs, and statutory charges like GST/STT — instead of one bundled number, giving investors more visibility into what they're actually paying for.

## Exit load

A fee charged if you redeem/withdraw before a minimum holding period (commonly around 1 year for many equity funds, though it varies by scheme) — usually a small percentage of the redemption amount, meant to discourage very short-term withdrawals. This is separate from any tax owed on the gains.

## Riskometer

SEBI requires every mutual fund scheme to display a "riskometer" — a visual gauge showing the fund's risk level (from "Low" to "Very High"), based on a standardized methodology, so investors can quickly gauge risk before investing rather than relying only on the fund's name or marketing.

## How mutual funds are taxed

- **Equity funds**: gains held over 12 months are long-term (LTCG) — taxed at 12.5%, with the first ₹1.25 lakh of gains per financial year exempt. Gains held 12 months or less are short-term (STCG), taxed at 20%.
- **Debt funds**: for units bought on or after 1 April 2023, all gains are taxed at your income tax slab rate regardless of holding period, with no indexation benefit. (Units bought before that date follow older, more favorable rules.)
- **Hybrid funds**: taxed as equity or debt depending on how much of the fund is actually invested in equity (generally, 65%+ in equity gets equity-fund tax treatment).

## How to actually invest (practical routes)

- **Directly on the AMC's own website/app** (cheapest — direct plan, no distributor commission).
- **Through registered platforms/apps** that offer direct plans (many now let you invest without extra commission).
- **Through a distributor, bank, or advisor** — convenient, but you end up in the regular plan with a slightly higher cost.
- **RTAs (Registrar and Transfer Agents)** like CAMS or KFintech — the back-end systems that actually process and record mutual fund transactions across AMCs; useful to know if a user asks where their consolidated statement comes from.

## Common misconceptions to correct

- "Mutual funds are guaranteed to give good returns" — false; returns depend entirely on market performance and are never guaranteed.
- "A mutual fund and a SIP are different things" — false; SIP is just a *method* of investing into a mutual fund, not a separate product.
- "Lower NAV = cheaper/better fund" — false, covered above.
- "Debt funds are risk-free" — false; they carry interest-rate risk and, for lower-rated bonds, credit/default risk. They're lower-risk than equity, not risk-free.
- "You need a large amount to start" — false; many funds allow SIPs starting from ₹500/month.
