# Budgeting & Emergency Fund — Complete Basics

## What is budgeting?

Budgeting means planning how your income gets spent, saved, and invested before the money actually arrives or leaves your account — instead of figuring it out after the fact when you check your bank balance. It's the foundation everything else in personal finance (SIP, FD, insurance) sits on top of, since none of those matter if you're spending more than you earn.

## A simple starting framework: the 50-30-20 rule

A common beginner framework for splitting take-home income:
- **50% — Needs**: rent, groceries, utilities, EMIs, insurance premiums — things you can't skip.
- **30% — Wants**: eating out, entertainment, shopping, travel — quality-of-life spending, not essential.
- **20% — Savings & investments**: emergency fund, SIPs, PPF, any goal-based saving.

This is a starting point, not a rigid rule — someone with high rent in a metro city may need to adjust the "needs" percentage upward and reduce "wants," while someone with low fixed costs might push savings well above 20%.

## Fixed vs variable expenses

- **Fixed expenses** — same amount every month (rent, EMI, subscriptions, insurance premiums). Easy to plan around since they don't change.
- **Variable expenses** — fluctuate month to month (groceries, fuel, entertainment). This is usually where budgeting has the most room to actually change behavior.

## What is an emergency fund?

Money set aside specifically to cover unexpected expenses or loss of income — a job loss, medical emergency, urgent home/vehicle repair — without needing to break investments, sell assets at a bad time, or take on high-interest debt.

## How much should an emergency fund hold?

A commonly recommended range is **3 to 6 months of essential monthly expenses** (not your full salary — just rent, EMIs, groceries, utilities, insurance — the "needs" bucket). People with less stable income (freelancers, business owners, commission-based roles) are often advised toward the higher end (6 months or more), while salaried employees with stable jobs may be comfortable at 3 months.

## Where to keep an emergency fund

The priority for this money is **accessibility, not returns** — you need to be able to get it quickly without penalty, so it's generally kept separate from long-term investments:
- **Savings account** — instantly accessible, but earns the lowest interest.
- **Liquid mutual funds** — slightly better returns than a savings account, can typically be redeemed within a day, low risk.
- **Sweep-in FD** — automatically moves excess savings-account balance into an FD for better interest, while remaining accessible.

**What NOT to use for an emergency fund:** equity mutual funds, stocks, or anything that can lose value right when you need to withdraw it, or has a lock-in period (like ELSS or a 5-year tax-saving FD) that prevents quick access.

## Building the fund if you're starting from zero

- Start smaller — even 1 month of expenses is a meaningful first milestone, not "all 6 months or nothing."
- Automate it — a fixed transfer to a separate account right after salary credit, before you have a chance to spend it, works the same discipline principle as a SIP.
- Treat it as a bill you pay yourself, not money left over at the end of the month.

## Common budgeting mistakes

- **Budgeting only "what's left over"** — instead of deciding savings first and spending what remains, many people spend first and save whatever's left, which is usually little to nothing.
- **No separate emergency fund** — using investments (which can lose value or have lock-ins) as an emergency fund instead of accessible, stable money.
- **Lifestyle inflation** — automatically increasing spending every time income rises, instead of increasing the savings percentage too.
- **Not tracking variable expenses** — fixed expenses are easy to see; variable/discretionary spending is where most budgets actually break down unnoticed.
