# Stocks — Complete Basics

## What is a stock?

A stock (or share) represents a small unit of ownership in a company. When you buy a stock, you own a proportional slice of that company — if the company grows and becomes more valuable, your shares typically become worth more; if it does poorly, they can lose value. Some companies also share profits with shareholders directly through dividends.

## How you actually buy and sell stocks

- **Demat account** — holds your shares electronically (like a bank account, but for securities instead of money). Required to own shares in India.
- **Trading account** — the account you actually use to place buy/sell orders on the stock exchange. Usually opened together with a demat account through a broker.
- **Broker** — a SEBI-registered entity (a bank, or a dedicated brokerage/app) that gives you access to place trades on the exchange. Full-service brokers offer research and advice at a higher cost; discount brokers offer lower-cost trading with fewer add-on services.
- **Stock exchanges** — in India, the two main ones are **NSE (National Stock Exchange)** and **BSE (Bombay Stock Exchange)**. Most stocks are listed on both, and prices are near-identical since they're linked by arbitrage.
- **PAN card and bank account** are required to open a demat/trading account, along with standard KYC (identity verification).

## How stock prices are decided

Prices move based on supply and demand — driven by company performance and outlook, broader economic conditions, industry trends, and market sentiment (including news, rumors, and investor psychology). There's no single formula; price is simply what a buyer and seller agree to trade at, in real time, on the exchange.

## Key ways companies group/classify stocks

- **By company size (market capitalization):** large-cap (biggest, most established), mid-cap (medium-sized, higher growth potential), small-cap (smaller, higher risk and potential reward).
- **By sector:** IT, banking/financial services, pharma, FMCG (consumer goods), auto, energy, etc.
- **Growth vs Value stocks:** growth stocks are expected to grow earnings faster than average (often reinvesting profits instead of paying dividends); value stocks are seen as undervalued relative to their fundamentals, often more established, sometimes paying steady dividends.
- **Blue-chip stocks** — a informal term for large, financially stable, well-established companies with a long track record — generally considered relatively lower-risk within equities (not risk-free).

## Key numbers/terms every beginner should know

- **Market capitalization (market cap)** — total value of a company's shares (share price × total number of shares outstanding). Used to classify company size.
- **P/E ratio (Price-to-Earnings)** — share price divided by earnings per share; a common (though incomplete) way to gauge whether a stock is expensive or cheap relative to its profits. A high P/E can mean the market expects strong future growth, or that the stock is overvalued — context matters, it's not a standalone signal.
- **EPS (Earnings Per Share)** — a company's profit divided by its number of outstanding shares.
- **Dividend** — a portion of company profit paid out directly to shareholders, usually per share, at the company's discretion (not guaranteed or fixed).
- **Dividend yield** — annual dividend per share divided by current share price, expressed as a percentage; shows the "income" return separate from any price appreciation.
- **52-week high/low** — the highest and lowest price a stock has traded at over the past year; commonly used as a quick reference point, not a prediction of future movement.
- **Circuit breaker** — a mechanism where the exchange automatically halts trading in a stock (or the whole market) if the price moves too much too fast in a single session, to control volatility.
- **IPO (Initial Public Offering)** — the first time a private company sells shares to the public and becomes listed on an exchange.

## How buying and selling actually settles

India follows a **T+1 settlement cycle** for most stocks — meaning if you buy or sell shares today, the shares/money are settled (transferred) the next trading day. Historically this was T+2 or longer; it has been progressively shortened over the years, so this is worth rechecking periodically since exchange rules do evolve.

## Costs involved in stock trading

- **Brokerage** — the fee your broker charges per trade; varies a lot between full-service and discount brokers, and by trade type (many discount brokers now offer very low or flat-fee brokerage on delivery trades).
- **STT (Securities Transaction Tax)** — a small tax charged by the government on every stock transaction, automatically collected at the time of the trade, at a rate that differs for buying vs selling and for delivery vs intraday trades.
- **Exchange transaction charges, GST, stamp duty, SEBI charges** — smaller regulatory/exchange fees added on top of brokerage, usually a fraction of a percent, bundled into your contract note after each trade.

## How stock market gains are taxed

- **Long-term capital gains (LTCG):** shares held for more than 12 months, taxed at **12.5%**, with the first **₹1.25 lakh of gains in a financial year exempt**. (Same rate structure as equity mutual funds, since both are equity-oriented instruments under current rules.)
- **Short-term capital gains (STCG):** shares held for 12 months or less, taxed at **20%**.
- **Dividends** are added to your total income and taxed at your income tax slab rate — they don't get any special lower rate.
- **Intraday trading** (buying and selling the same stock within the same day, never taking delivery) is treated differently — as **speculative business income**, taxed at your slab rate, not as capital gains, and reported separately when filing.

## Investing vs Trading — an important distinction

- **Investing** generally means buying stocks with a longer time horizon (years), based on a company's fundamentals and growth prospects, aiming to build wealth over time.
- **Trading** (including intraday, swing trading) means buying and selling over much shorter timeframes, trying to profit from short-term price movements — this requires more active time, skill, and carries meaningfully higher risk, and most beginners are better served starting with investing rather than trading.

## Who regulates the stock market

**SEBI (Securities and Exchange Board of India)** regulates stock exchanges, brokers, listed companies' disclosures, and investor protection rules. **RBI** separately regulates monetary policy and banking, which indirectly affects markets (e.g., interest rate changes) but doesn't directly regulate stock trading.

## Common beginner mistakes/misconceptions

- "A cheap stock (low price per share) is a good buy" — false; share price alone says nothing about value — a ₹50 stock and a ₹5,000 stock can both be over- or under-valued depending on the underlying business.
- "Stocks always go up in the long run" — not guaranteed; individual companies can permanently decline or go to zero, which is why diversification (not putting everything into one stock) matters.
- "You need a lot of money to start" — false; you can buy even a single share of many companies, and fractional/small investing options are increasingly available.
- "Following tips/rumors is a valid strategy" — risky; acting on unverified tips (especially on social media or messaging groups) is a common way beginners lose money, and can also run into regulatory issues around market manipulation if such tips involve insider information.
- "Trading and investing are the same thing" — false, as covered above — very different risk profiles and skill requirements.
