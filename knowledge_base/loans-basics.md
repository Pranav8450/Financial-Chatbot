# Loans — Complete Basics (Home, Personal, Education)

## Core loan concepts everyone should know

- **Principal** — the actual amount you borrow.
- **Interest rate** — the cost of borrowing, expressed as an annual percentage.
- **EMI (Equated Monthly Installment)** — the fixed monthly payment that covers both interest and a portion of the principal, structured so the loan is fully repaid by the end of the tenure.
- **Tenure** — how long you have to repay the loan. Longer tenure means lower EMI but more total interest paid over the loan's life.
- **Fixed vs floating interest rate** — fixed stays the same for the loan term; floating moves with market/RBI rate changes (can go up or down over time). Most home loans in India are floating rate.
- **Collateral/security** — an asset pledged against the loan (like the house itself for a home loan). Loans with collateral (secured loans) generally have lower interest rates than loans without collateral (unsecured loans), since the lender has lower risk.

## Home loans

- **What they're for:** buying, constructing, or renovating a residential property. The property itself typically serves as collateral.
- **Interest deduction (Section 24(b)):** interest paid on a home loan for a **self-occupied property** is deductible up to **₹2,00,000 per financial year**, under Section 24(b) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 — this limit was **not changed in Budget 2026** despite widespread expectation that it would rise, so it remains ₹2 lakh. For a **let-out (rented) property**, there's no upper cap on the interest deduction itself, though the amount you can offset against other income in a single year is capped at ₹2 lakh, with the rest carried forward.
- **Principal repayment** is separately eligible under Section 80C/123 (up to the shared ₹1.5 lakh limit covered in the SIP and FD articles).
- **This interest deduction is available only under the old tax regime** for self-occupied property; under the new (default) regime, home loan interest deductions are generally limited to let-out properties only.
- **First-time buyer bonus schemes (80EE, 80EEA)** offered extra deductions in the past, but both windows have closed (80EE applied only to loans sanctioned April 2016-March 2017; 80EEA to loans sanctioned April 2019-March 2022) — if you took a loan in those specific windows you may still be claiming it, but these aren't available for new loans today.

## Personal loans

- **What they're for:** general-purpose borrowing (medical expenses, weddings, travel, debt consolidation) — no collateral required, and no restriction on how the money is used.
- **Higher interest rates than secured loans** — since there's no collateral, personal loan interest rates are meaningfully higher than home loans (often roughly double or more), reflecting the higher risk to the lender.
- **No tax deduction** in most cases — unlike home or education loans, personal loan interest generally doesn't come with a tax benefit, unless the loan proceeds were specifically used for a purpose that itself qualifies (e.g., using a personal loan for home renovation could, in some cases, qualify similarly to a home improvement loan — this is a nuanced area worth checking with a tax professional for a specific situation).
- **Best used for:** genuine short-term needs where you have clear repayment capacity — not for discretionary spending, given the high interest cost.

## Education loans

- **What they're for:** funding higher education (in India or abroad) — tuition, living expenses, and related costs.
- **Interest deduction (Section 80E):** the **entire interest paid** on an education loan is deductible with **no upper limit** — unlike most other deductions which are capped. This is one of the more generous provisions in the tax code.
- Available for a **maximum of 8 consecutive years** from when repayment begins, or until the interest is fully repaid, whichever comes first.
- Can be claimed for a loan taken for yourself, your spouse, your children, or a legal ward — not just for your own education.
- Only the **interest** portion qualifies under 80E; principal repayment isn't deductible under this section (though it may separately qualify under 80C in some cases).
- **Old tax regime only** — from 1 April 2026, this is referenced as **Section 129** under the Income Tax Act, 2025, with the same substantive rules.

## Choosing between loan tenure lengths

- **Shorter tenure:** higher EMI, but significantly less total interest paid over the loan's life.
- **Longer tenure:** lower, more manageable EMI, but meaningfully more total interest paid.
- A common approach: choose the shortest tenure whose EMI is comfortably affordable, and consider prepaying (paying extra toward principal) when you have surplus funds, which reduces total interest without committing to a higher fixed EMI from day one.

## Prepayment and foreclosure

- **Prepayment** — paying extra toward the principal ahead of schedule, reducing the outstanding loan amount and future interest.
- **Foreclosure** — paying off the entire remaining loan early.
- For **floating-rate home loans**, RBI rules generally prohibit banks from charging a prepayment/foreclosure penalty to individual borrowers — this is a meaningful consumer protection worth knowing, and a common reason floating-rate home loans are often preferred over fixed-rate ones for borrowers who expect to prepay.

## Common misconceptions

- "Lower EMI always means a better loan" — a lower EMI from a longer tenure often means paying significantly more total interest; always compare total interest paid, not just the monthly figure.
- "All loan interest is tax-deductible" — false; only specific loan types (home, education, and business loans in certain cases) have interest deductions, and only under the old tax regime in most cases. Personal loans generally don't.
- "Prepaying a loan always has a penalty" — not true for floating-rate home loans to individual borrowers, per RBI rules; always check the specific loan's terms since rules can differ for fixed-rate loans or other loan types.
