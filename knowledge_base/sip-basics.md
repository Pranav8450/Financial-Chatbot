# What is a SIP (Systematic Investment Plan)?

## The basic idea

A SIP is a way of investing a fixed amount of money into a mutual fund at regular intervals — usually monthly — instead of investing one large amount all at once. Think of it like a recurring deposit, but the money goes into mutual funds (which invest in stocks, bonds, or a mix) instead of sitting in a fixed-interest bank account.

For example, instead of investing ₹60,000 in one go, you could set up a SIP of ₹5,000 per month for 12 months. Each month, that fixed amount is automatically deducted from your bank account and used to buy units of the mutual fund you chose.

## How it actually works

1. You pick a mutual fund scheme.
2. You choose an amount and a date each month (e.g., ₹2,000 on the 5th of every month).
3. Every month on that date, the amount is auto-debited from your bank account.
4. That money buys units of the mutual fund at that day's price (called the NAV — Net Asset Value).
5. Over time, you accumulate units, and your investment grows or shrinks with the fund's performance.

## Why people choose SIP over investing a lump sum

- **Rupee cost averaging** — since you're buying at different prices every month (sometimes high, sometimes low), you naturally average out your purchase cost over time instead of risking putting all your money in at a market high.
- **Discipline** — it's automatic, so you're not relying on remembering to invest or deciding "is now a good time."
- **Lower entry barrier** — many SIPs allow starting with small amounts (some funds allow as low as ₹500/month), so you don't need a large sum to begin.
- **Power of compounding** — staying invested over many years lets your returns generate their own returns, which is where SIPs tend to show the most benefit — but this requires a long time horizon, not a short one.

## What a SIP is NOT

- It is **not a fixed-return product** — unlike a Fixed Deposit, the returns are not guaranteed. Your money is invested in the market (via the mutual fund), so its value can go up or down.
- It is **not a separate investment type** — SIP is just a *method* of investing into a mutual fund. The risk and return depend entirely on which mutual fund scheme you choose (equity funds are higher risk/return, debt funds are lower risk/return).
- It does **not guarantee profit** — even with rupee cost averaging, if the market is down for a long stretch, a SIP can still show a loss.

## Common beginner questions

**Can I stop a SIP anytime?**
Yes, SIPs are generally flexible — you can pause, stop, or change the amount, though this depends on the fund house's specific rules.

**Is SIP only for stocks?**
No — you can do a SIP into equity mutual funds (stock-based), debt mutual funds (bond-based), or hybrid funds (a mix), depending on your risk appetite.

**How is SIP different from a Recurring Deposit (RD)?**
An RD is a bank product with a fixed, guaranteed interest rate. A SIP invests in the market through a mutual fund, so returns are variable and not guaranteed — but historically have the potential to outperform RDs over long periods, along with more risk.

## Types of SIP

- **Regular/Fixed SIP** — the standard version described above: same amount, same date, every month.
- **Step-up (or Top-up) SIP** — you set your SIP to automatically increase by a fixed amount or percentage at regular intervals (e.g., increase by 10% every year). This lets your investment grow along with your income, without needing to manually change it.
- **Flexible SIP** — allows you to change the installment amount each month within a set range, useful if your monthly savings capacity varies.
- **Perpetual SIP** — has no fixed end date; it continues until you actively stop it. Most SIPs today are set up this way by default.
- **Fixed-tenure SIP** — you set a specific number of installments (e.g., 60 months) and it stops automatically after that.
- **Trigger SIP** — installments are linked to a market condition or index level rather than a fixed date (less common, and generally not recommended for beginners since it adds complexity without proven benefit).

## How SIP is taxed

*(Figures below verified against current sources as of September 2026 — India moved to the new Income Tax Act 2025 from 1 April 2026, which renumbered sections but largely kept the same limits and rates.)*

- Each SIP installment is treated as a **separate, individual investment** for tax purposes — meaning it has its own purchase date and its own holding period, even though they're all part of the "same" SIP.
- **Equity mutual funds:** units held for more than 12 months qualify as long-term capital gains (LTCG), taxed at **12.5%**, with the first **₹1.25 lakh of gains in a financial year exempt from tax**. Units held 12 months or less are short-term capital gains (STCG), taxed at **20%**.
- **Debt mutual funds:** since a 2023 rule change, gains on debt fund units purchased on or after **1 April 2023** are treated as short-term capital gains **regardless of how long you hold them**, and are added to your income and taxed at your regular income tax slab rate — there is no separate long-term rate and no indexation benefit anymore. (Units bought before that date still follow the older rules.)
- Because each installment has its own holding period, redeeming a SIP investment can mean some units qualify for long-term (lower-tax) treatment while more recently purchased units don't — this trips up a lot of beginners.

## Missed or bounced installment

- If the auto-debit fails (insufficient bank balance, etc.), most fund houses simply skip that month's installment — there's typically no direct penalty from the mutual fund.
- However, your bank may charge an auto-debit/ECS bounce fee, and repeated failures can sometimes lead the fund house to pause or cancel the SIP after a certain number of consecutive misses (varies by fund house).

## Exit load

- Some mutual funds charge an **exit load** — a fee for withdrawing your investment before a minimum holding period (commonly 1 year, though it varies by scheme).
- This is usually a small percentage (e.g., 1%) of the redemption amount and is meant to discourage short-term withdrawals.
- Exit load is separate from capital gains tax — you could owe both if you withdraw early from certain funds.

## ELSS — tax-saving SIP

- **ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme)** funds are a category of equity mutual funds that qualify for a tax deduction of **up to ₹1.5 lakh per financial year**, under what was Section 80C of the old Income Tax Act, 1961. From 1 April 2026, this same deduction continues under **Section 123 (read with Schedule XV)** of the new Income Tax Act, 2025 — same ₹1.5 lakh combined limit (shared across all 80C-type instruments like PPF, EPF, life insurance, etc., not a separate limit just for ELSS), just a new section number.
- **Important:** this deduction is only available if you're filing under the **old tax regime**. It is not available under the new (default) tax regime.
- You can do a SIP into an ELSS fund just like any other mutual fund, getting the benefit of both disciplined investing and a tax deduction.
- ELSS comes with a **mandatory lock-in period of 3 years** — the shortest lock-in among all Section 80C/123 options — and importantly, in a SIP, each individual installment is locked in for 3 years from *its own* investment date, not from when you started the SIP. So your money doesn't all become withdrawable at once.

## A worked example (illustration only, not a guarantee)

If someone invests ₹5,000/month via SIP for 10 years, and the investment grows at an assumed average annual return of 12% (a commonly used illustrative rate for equity mutual funds — actual returns can be higher, lower, or negative in any given period):

- Total amount invested over 10 years: ₹6,00,000 (₹5,000 × 120 months)
- Approximate value at the end, with compounding: roughly ₹11.5–11.6 lakh

This is purely a mathematical illustration of compounding — real mutual fund returns fluctuate and are never guaranteed. This kind of example should always be labeled clearly as illustrative when shown to users, never presented as an expected or promised outcome.
